ʿAlī ibn Sulaymān al-Muqaddasī (fl. late 11th – early 12th c.) and his Commentary on Genesis

Who was Ali?

ʿAlī ibn Sulaymān — known in Hebrew as Eli ben Shelomo (ʿEli ben Solomon) and in Arabic as Abū ʾl-Ḥasan ʿAlī ibn Sulaymān al-Muqaddasī, "the Jerusalemite" — was one of the last great Karaite scholars of medieval Jerusalem. He flourished in the closing decades of the eleventh century and continued working into the early twelfth; a note in his own hand is dated to 1103 CE.

His teachers were the leading Karaite intellectuals of late-eleventh-century Palestine and Egypt. Jeshua ben Judah (Yeshuʿah ben Yehudah, Abū ʾl-Faraj Furqān ibn Asad) was probably his master in Bible. Sahl ben Faḍl al-Tustarī (Yashar ben Ḥesed), the Karaite theologian, composed his treatise Maqdisiyyāt in answer to questions Ali had put to him — so he was, in effect, Ali's master in kalām (speculative theology). Ali himself produced a stream of works: four Pentateuch commentaries, a Psalms commentary, a famous Hebrew lexicon called al-Agrōn (for which contemporaries knew him as ṣāḥib al-agrōn, "the man of the Agrōn"), a Karaite legal compendium, philosophical-theological tracts, and an anti-Islamic polemic. Almost everything he wrote was an abridgement (Arabic: intizāʿ, ikhtiṣār, mukhtaṣar) of earlier Karaite work. His gift was synthesis, not invention.

Sometime before the First Crusade reached Jerusalem in 1099, Ali left the city — almost certainly for Fustat (Old Cairo), where Karaite scholarship was beginning to take root. He was among the last of the avelei Tziyon — the "Mourners of Zion," the Karaite community that had made Jerusalem the spiritual and intellectual center of Karaite Judaism for two centuries. With the Crusader sack, that community ended. Ali's writing carries the world that produced it.

What is the Commentary on Genesis?

The text translated here is the Genesis portion of one of Ali's four Pentateuch commentaries — specifically, the intizāʿ ("digest") he made from Abū ʾl-Faraj Hārūn's abridgement of Joseph ibn Nūḥ's commentary on the Pentateuch. Hārūn, in the early eleventh century, had himself abridged Yūsuf ibn Nūḥ's late-tenth-century original. Ali's autograph manuscript survives in two pieces — Russian National Library Yevr.-Arab. I 4423 and Jewish Theological Seminary 9334 — and the Genesis section was edited and published by Solomon L. Skoss in Philadelphia in 1928. Skoss's edition is the base text for this translation. What we read is therefore three layers deep: a tradition of Karaite Bible-reading that began with Ibn Nūḥ, was honed by Hārūn, and reached us in the form Ali gave it.

How the commentary works

The commentary is selective. Ali does not gloss every verse — he stops where he has something to say. Of Genesis's fifty chapters, he treats twenty-eight. He skips most of the genealogical material, the second half of the Flood narrative, large stretches of the Abraham and Jacob cycles, and most of the Joseph story.

What he treats, he treats in a single recurring pattern:

  • A Hebrew lemma is quoted (e.g., bereshit — "in the beginning").
  • A gloss follows in Judeo-Arabic, often introduced by wa-qawluhu ("and his saying"), al-aqrab ("the closer [reading]"), or qīla… wa-qīla ("it has been said… and it has been said").
  • A dialectical question-and-answer may follow in the form fa-in qīla… kāna al-jawāb… ("if it be said… the answer is…"). This is the Karaite masʾala — the rationalist genre of weighing one possible reading against another. It is the heart of Karaite exegesis.

The voice is dense. Ali presupposes a reader who knows the Hebrew text well, who is comfortable with Arabic grammatical terminology, and who is ready to follow a fast-moving argument. Some passages are extended masʾala-style discussion; some are short grammatical or lexical notes; a handful are a Hebrew word followed by a single Arabic gloss.

The Karaite intellectual frame

Ali wrote within the Karaite engagement with Muʿtazilite rationalist theology — the speculative tradition that anchored Islamic and Jewish kalām in this period. Several characteristic Karaite positions surface in the Genesis commentary:

  • A rejection of al-ṣarfa — the Muslim theological claim that the Qurʾān's inimitability comes not from anything in the text itself but from God's "diverting" people from being able to compose its like. In the Babel pericope (Genesis 11), Ali argues that human language and human intellect themselves bear the marks of divine ordering.
  • A rejection of transmigration of souls, also at Genesis 11, where Ali considers and dismisses the doctrine.
  • A pre-existent-sin theodicy, especially at Genesis 18 — the proposal, associated with Muʿtazilite-leaning Karaites, that apparent injustices in earthly life resolve in a moral economy that takes prior states of the soul into account.

These are not asides. They are how Ali reads the text. Where the Hebrew Bible raises a question that Karaite kalām has addressed, Ali brings the kalām answer into the commentary.

How this edition was made

The translation was prepared from Skoss 1928. The editorial principles are simple:

  • Preserve Ali's voice. We render his parataxis as parataxis, his short sentences short, his dialectical structure intact. We do not smooth.
  • Preserve his quirks. Ali sometimes cites a verse by its number when he means a nearby verse; he sometimes refers to an earlier verse as coming "after" a later one; he sometimes lights on a textual reading that disagrees with the Masoretic text we know. We preserve every such case as he gives it and flag each in a footnote, so the reader can see Ali's text rather than a corrected version of it.
  • Footnote for context, not correction. Footnotes explain technical Arabic terms, situate sectarian arguments, identify quoted authorities, and note manuscript lacunae. The translation stays out of the way.
  • A glossary for recurring terms. Many of Ali's technical terms — ḥukm, maʿrifa, dalāla, ʿilla, naqḍ ʿāda — recur dozens of times. We define each once in the Glossary sheet and refer back to it from the footnotes.

Manuscript lacunae are marked with […]. A paragraph break in Ali's manuscript is noted with "End" on the relevant row. Where a chapter contains only a Hebrew lemma with no surviving Arabic gloss — at Genesis 37:1 and Genesis 44:18 — we render the lemma and explain the absence in a footnote.
